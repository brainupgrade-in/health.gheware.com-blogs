Diabetic Foot Care Prevention in India: 2026 Guide

Diabetic foot complications remain a leading cause of hospitalization in India, yet up to 85% of ulcers are preventable with consistent daily care. This guide outlines evidence-based strategies for Indian patients to protect their mobility and quality of life.

The Urgent Need for Proactive Foot Care in India

Diabetes is often described as a systemic disease, but its impact is frequently felt most acutely in the extremities. In India, where the prevalence of diabetes is rising rapidly, diabetic foot complications represent a significant public health challenge. According to the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR), diabetes affects a substantial portion of the adult population, with peripheral neuropathy and peripheral arterial disease being common comorbidities. These conditions compromise sensation and blood flow, turning minor injuries into severe ulcers.

The consequences are severe. Studies cited by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) indicate that non-traumatic lower limb amputations are highly prevalent in low- and middle-income countries, with India bearing a disproportionate burden. However, the narrative is not one of inevitability. Evidence-based foot care protocols, when followed consistently, can reduce the risk of ulceration by over 85%. Health Gheware emphasizes that prevention is not just about medical intervention but about daily, mindful habits that integrate seamlessly into Indian lifestyles.

Understanding the anatomy of risk is the first step. The feet are under constant pressure and exposure to environmental hazards. For an individual with diabetes, the protective mechanisms of the skin and nerves are diminished. Therefore, a structured approach to foot care is not optional; it is a critical component of diabetes management. This guide outlines the specific actions, dietary considerations, and monitoring techniques recommended by leading health authorities for 2026.

Daily Inspection and Hygiene: The First Line of Defense

Daily foot inspection is the single most effective practice for preventing diabetic foot ulcers. Because neuropathy can dull sensation, patients may not feel cuts, blisters, or sores until they are already infected. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommends that individuals with diabetes inspect their feet every day for cuts, blisters, redness, swelling, or changes in color or temperature. In the Indian context, where footwear such as chappals or sandals is common, the risk of foreign body injury is heightened.

Effective hygiene goes beyond simple washing. The skin of diabetic patients is often prone to dryness and cracking, which creates entry points for bacteria. Therefore, moisturizing is essential, but it must be done correctly. Moisturizer should be applied to the tops and bottoms of the feet to prevent cracking, but never between the toes, where excess moisture can lead to fungal infections—a common issue in India’s humid climate.

  • Wash feet daily with lukewarm water, ensuring the temperature is tested with the elbow or a thermometer to prevent burns, as heat sensitivity may be reduced.
  • Dry feet thoroughly, especially between the toes, to prevent fungal growth.
  • Apply a urea-based or fragrance-free moisturizer to dry areas, avoiding interdigital spaces.
  • Trim toenails straight across to prevent ingrown toenails, which can become infected.
  • Never walk barefoot, even indoors, to protect against unnoticed injuries from sharp objects or hot surfaces.

Visual inspection should be complemented by tactile checks. If sight is impaired, use a mirror to check the soles of the feet. Additionally, family members can assist in this process, ensuring that no area is missed. Regular removal of calluses by a healthcare professional is advised, as self-removal can lead to accidental cuts and subsequent infection.

Footwear and Environmental Safety

Footwear plays a pivotal role in diabetic foot health. The choice of shoes can either protect the feet or cause chronic trauma leading to ulcers. In India, the transition from traditional footwear to synthetic shoes often introduces issues related to fit and material breathability. The ADA guidelines stress the importance of wearing properly fitted shoes and socks at all times. Loose or tight shoes can cause friction, leading to blisters and ulcers.

It is crucial to check the inside of shoes before putting them on. Small pebbles, seams, or rough edges can cause significant damage to insensitive feet. For patients with existing foot deformities or severe neuropathy, custom-made therapeutic footwear may be recommended by a podiatrist or orthopedist. These shoes are designed to redistribute pressure and prevent high-risk areas from bearing weight.

Footwear Feature Recommendation Rationale
Sole Thickness Thick, flexible, and shock-absorbing Reduces pressure on the sole and prevents injury from sharp objects.
Toe Box Wide and deep Prevents crowding of toes and reduces friction on bunions or hammertoes.
Material Breathable leather or mesh Allows air circulation, reducing sweat and fungal risk.
Fit Snug but not tight Prevents slipping and rubbing while allowing natural foot movement.

Additionally, socks should be made of moisture-wicking materials like cotton or wool blends, avoiding synthetic fibers that trap heat and moisture. Seams should be minimal or seamless to prevent irritation. Changing socks daily and keeping them clean is essential for maintaining skin integrity.

Nutrition, Blood Sugar Control, and Professional Care

While daily hygiene and footwear are immediate actions, long-term foot health depends on systemic control. Persistent hyperglycemia damages blood vessels and nerves, accelerating the progression of peripheral arterial disease (PAD) and neuropathy. The ICMR emphasizes that maintaining target blood glucose levels is paramount in preventing these complications. For many patients, an HbA1c target of less than 7% is recommended, though this should be individualized based on age and comorbidities.

Dietary choices in the Indian context often involve high carbohydrate intake from rice and wheat. Balancing this with fiber-rich vegetables, lentils, and whole grains can help stabilize blood sugar levels. Furthermore, adequate hydration supports skin health, preventing the dryness that leads to fissures. Omega-3 fatty acids, found in flaxseeds and walnuts, may support vascular health, although patients should consult their doctors before making significant dietary changes.

Regular professional foot exams are non-negotiable. The ADA recommends a comprehensive foot examination at least annually for all patients with diabetes. However, for those with risk factors such as previous ulcers, amputation, neuropathy, or PAD, more frequent examinations—every 3 to 6 months—are advised. During these visits, healthcare providers assess sensation using a monofilament test, check reflexes, and evaluate pulses to detect early signs of PAD.

Smoking cessation is another critical factor. Smoking constricts blood vessels, worsening circulation and impairing wound healing. Patients who smoke are at a significantly higher risk for foot ulcers and amputation. Quitting smoking, supported by counseling or nicotine replacement therapy, is one of the most impactful steps for improving long-term outcomes.

By integrating these practices—daily inspection, appropriate footwear, strict glycemic control, and regular professional care—individuals with diabetes can significantly reduce their risk of complications. These steps are not burdensome but rather simple habits that yield profound benefits. For personalized advice, always consult your doctor or a certified diabetes educator.
